intro: 'Although the application of artificial intelligence (AI) in chemistry is advancing rapidly, its practical implementation and governance in the context of chemical weapons control remain at an early stage. Rather than consolidating existing tools, many AI papers introduce new models, creating a fragmented and largely unregulated landscape. This poses risks for chemical weapons control, as it could facilitate the discovery of novel synthesis routes for chemical warfare agents, for example. At the same time, promising AI applications for verification, forensics, training, and mission planning remain unimplemented, creating an “execution gap” between recognized opportunities and operational practice. While the Chemical Weapons Convention (CWC) remains a robust instrument and AI does not currently pose an immediate existential threat, preserving this situation will require systematic monitoring of AI developments, capacity building, engagement with developers on dual-use safeguards, and efforts to overcome the political obstacles that hamper effective implementation and regulation.'

<Leadin>Artificial intelligence </Leadin>is playing an increasingly significant role in chemical research. However, recent developments raise questions about the sustainability of this trend toward widespread and long-term use in research, as well as its implications for the control of chemical weapons. While the 2025 Monitor primarily outlined the emerging possibilities offered by AI agents based on large language models (LLMs), we can now provide a more nuanced assessment of which of these tools are likely to become established in research practice. Although the number of AI-related chemistry publications is increasing, rather than consolidating existing applications, many studies introduce or test new AI models, contributing to a fragmentation of AI tool use. The large number of models makes it more difficult to evaluate them systematically, develop appropriate safeguards to address their dual use potential, and integrate them reliably into research practice.
