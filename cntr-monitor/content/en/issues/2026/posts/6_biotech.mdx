title: 'Genetic Engineering Attribution: Contribution to Verification and Practical Limitations'
intro: 'This chapter examines whether genetic engineering attribution (GEA) can meaningfully contribute to biosecurity and verification. It asks what current methods can actually infer from engineered biological material, how far results from curated datasets (e.g., plasmid repositories) can be applied to real-world incidents, and what this means for verification and compliance. The analysis suggests that sequence-based attribution may support screening, prioritization, and the narrowing of hypotheses, but that its value remains limited by incomplete data, uneven metadata, and the gap between repository benchmarks and realistic forensic settings. For this reason, the priorities are to improve data standards, evaluate attribution methods under more realistic conditions, and use sequence-based results only in combination with other forms of evidence.'

<Leadin>Efforts to </Leadin>attribute the origin of biological agents are not new. Microbial forensics was developed to investigate biological incidents, identify the agent involved, where it came from, and who may have been responsible. While microbes have been used for harmful purposes for centuries, the 2001 anthrax letter attacks in the United States gave particular urgency to the development of forensic capabilities to support investigation and attribution. These events highlighted the need for analytical tools and knowledge bases that could provide investigative leads, identify likely sources, and help establish responsibility with a high degree of scientific confidence. In practice, this meant combining genomic analysis with epidemiological information, environmental and clinical samples, and documented chains of custody.
