<Leadin>Drones, or uncrewed </Leadin>aerial vehicles (UAVs), are still dominating the battlefields in Ukraine and have had a significant impact on the way the war is fought. Last year’s update focused on the issue of “swarming” and how to circumvent the problem of electronic warfare, for example by using ultra-thin fiber-optic cables. However, since last year, it has become increasingly clear just how profoundly drones are changing the very nature of warfare. Rather than a continuous front, the contact zone between the Russian invaders and Ukrainian defenders has become a collection of small outposts, each manned by two or three soldiers, as drone attacks have made it too dangerous to concentrate troops in a single location along the front. While artillery remains highly important, it is increasingly being replaced and constrained by drones. At the same time, the “killing zone”—the strip of land on either side of the front within which the enemy can strike—has continuously expanded as UAV ranges have increased, driven by advances in battery technology and drone design, including winged quadcopters. While combat missions are still the most important application, new drone uses have become more relevant, including high-precision mining from the air and the delivery of supplies to otherwise unreachable frontline outposts. When it comes to innovation, cycles have shrunk from years to months, and sometimes even weeks, while superior technology is rapidly replicated by the opposing side. Consequently, Ukraine has upscaled production capacity again this year. Last year we reported an estimated production of 2.2 million drones for 2025, with the actual production being close to four million. For 2026, Ukraine is expected to produce at least seven million units, with an estimated industrial capacity of ten million. Cheaper, mass-produced drones have become increasingly important, as drones are being deployed in swarms more frequently than they were last year. Although Iran’s in-house technological developments were long dismissed as laughable, the country has now emerged as one of the leading developers of what are known as “one-way attack drones,” which, in terms of their use, are less like drones and more like cruise missiles (see CNTR Monitor 2024). The best-known of these “drones” is the Shahed 136, which has been used in attacks against both Israel and American forces, but is also being launched in large numbers by Russia against Ukraine. In a short space of time, the U.S. even fielded its own low-cost, mass-producible variant of this class of drone, the FLM-136 Low-cost Unmanned Combat Attack System (LUCAS), which was first deployed in the war against Iran.
