<Leadin>Since radioactivity </Leadin>is a defining feature of nuclear materials, the verification of nuclear inventory and facilities often relies on radiation detection devices. Simultaneously, radiation protection requires these materials to be biologically shielded to ensure safe operations, making reliable measurements—and hence verification—more challenging. In addition to the dangers that radioactive materials pose to living organisms and the environment, they can also be used for the—prohibited—construction of nuclear weapons, ranging from dirty bombs to nuclear warheads. Consequently, the whereabouts of nuclear material must be meticulously documented and verified. This is partially due to the need for long-term management of increasing quantities of spent nuclear fuel (SNF) in the absence of a clear pathway toward its final disposal. Simultaneously, nuclear energy is experiencing a global resurgence as a potential means of reconciling demands for energy sovereignty and low-carbon electricity. New nuclear energy programs have been launched by several countries, including Poland, Türkiye, and Egypt, while existing programs are being expanded or revived following earlier phase-out decisions. One major development is the emergence of new deployment concepts, including fleets of small modular reactors (SMRs) and microreactors for decentralized, industrial, transportable, or mobile nuclear power—potentially even for the maritime propulsion of cargo vessels. In addition to this, new novel advanced reactors (NARs or Gen IV reactors) using different fuel forms and coolants are also being developed, particularly by nuclear power start-ups in the U.S. These new concepts challenge existing safeguards measures designed to ensure non-proliferation: larger, more widely dispersed fleets of reactors make inspection logistics more challenging, longer refueling cycles or sealed cores prevent access to fuel elements, and new coolants that cannot be detected by existing measurement techniques further hinder verification efforts. Accommodating these new concepts, designs, and deployment approaches would require major modifications to current safeguards methods as well as additional personnel. New nuclear verification techniques are therefore needed to ensure that current safeguards remain effective, and research and academic institutions are investigating such approaches. These efforts depend on improvements in signal detection and data-processing techniques resulting from ongoing R&D. Non-destructive assay (NDA) approaches for the passive or active detection of nuclear materials are of particular interest because they offer nonintrusive procedures that can minimize the burden and risks to operators. Therefore, the development of novel techniques capable of enhancing existing surveillance systems through nondestructive, continuous, or remote monitoring is particularly promising in terms of future verification tools for nuclear facilities and material.
