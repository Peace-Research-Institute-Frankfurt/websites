title: 'Beyond the Black Box: Conditions for Credible Use of Artificial Intelligence in Verification'
intro: 'This chapter examines whether the often-cited “black box” character of AI constitutes a fundamental obstacle to its use in arms control verification or whether—and under what conditions—AI can become a credible verification tool. It argues that verification has always involved balancing transparency against the protection of sensitive information, and that the central challenge is not explainability itself, but credibility in a limited-trust environment. The chapter identifies the key technical and institutional requirements for credible AI use, including data protection, integrity, reproducibility, decentralization, transparency, and secure infrastructure. It further examines the governance trade-offs that arise when these requirements are negotiated and concludes that AI can strengthen verification regimes when embedded in robust institutional arrangements and accepted by participating actors.'

<Leadin>Across almost all </Leadin>technical and administrative domains, there is a clear trend toward the increasing use of artificial intelligence (AI). Data volumes are growing, sensors are proliferating, and political decision-making is increasingly expected to be informed by continuous monitoring and rapid analysis. Arms control, disarmament, and non-proliferation verification are not exempt from this development. Not every verification procedure requires AI support, but it can help address some of the challenges that verification regimes encounter and can serve as an enabling factor for the deployment of modern technologies. Some of these challenges include an ever-growing number of facilities needing to be safeguarded by a limited human workforce, the increasing availability of sensors for verification purposes, and the accelerating pace of geopolitical developments requiring fast and reliable compliance assessments. Here, AI is particularly attractive as it promises to support exactly those tasks that are resource intensive for humans: screening large amounts of sensor data, detecting anomalies, aggregating heterogeneous information streams, and accelerating otherwise lengthy verification procedures to make verification more efficient, responsive, and potentially more objective.

But verification is a highly political endeavor. Introducing AI into its processes raises questions about the explainability of the data processing, the credibility of its outcomes, the development and negotiation of the underlying algorithms, the selection and balance of the data used to train the AI, and even potentially unequal access of different participating actors to advanced models or computing infrastructure—all of which will be discussed in the following sections.
