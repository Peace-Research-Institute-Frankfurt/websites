title: Societal Verification
intro: 'Against the backdrop of the erosion of formal verification environments and the decline in treaty-based options, societal verification has attracted renewed interest. Societal verification—a concept that assigns non-state actors, including NGOs, think tanks, and researchers, a role in arms control and non-proliferation monitoring using open-source information—holds promise for practical application. Today, non-state experts with the expertise to analyze satellite imagery produce information on specific proliferation hot spots—largely states perceived as Western adversaries—that is then widely disseminated. Based on interviews with state officials and non-state analysts, this contribution provides an overview of the opportunities societal verification activities offer, as well as the practical limitations faced by those implementing the concept.'

<Leadin>With the NPT </Leadin>under increasing strain, the JCPOA defunct, and New START having expired in February 2026, formal verification mechanisms are steadily disappearing. For arms control verification—the process of collecting, analyzing, and checking data to help assess whether a state is fulfilling its obligations under an international agreement or treaty—knowledge production using national technical means (NTM) and contractually regulated inspection regimes continues to play a key role. However, analyses by independent non-state actors—including NGOs, think tanks, and independent analysts—using open-source intelligence (OSINT) have increasingly come to the fore, broadening the debate. This information has largely been disseminated through open-access reports and analysis, media statements, and other forms of public engagement concerning states such as North Korea, Iran, and China. While non-state actors do not officially verify arms control agreements or perform actual verification tasks, they do gather verification-relevant information and share it publicly. In democratic states, societal verification has enabled OSINT analysts outside the government–intelligence nexus to contribute to the dissemination of knowledge, using, for example, commercial satellite images, trade data, and shipping information. In doing so, they provide the public with information that complements government accounts, at times even revealing previously undisclosed developments, including Iranian reconstruction activities following U.S. and Israeli strikes and on-the-ground activities in China related to the construction of missile silos.
